> Why do we mistake continuity for intimacy?

We often imagine intimacy as something linear. A conversation becomes closeness, closeness becomes familiarity, and familiarity creates an expectation of continuity. When someone opens themselves to us, we unconsciously expect that version of them to remain available.

> But perhaps we have mistaken continuity for intimacy.

A person can be deeply open with us one day and distant the next. They can tell us things they have never told anyone, then spend the following week speaking only about ordinary things. They can disappear into themselves without having withdrawn what they once shared.

They haven't withdrawn their intimacy. They simply aren't in that state today.

We forget that people are not fixed conditions.

> state ≠ identity

> state ≠ relationship

The way someone exists with us today does not necessarily define who they are, nor does it rewrite what existed between us yesterday.

> Continuity isn't the same as consistency.

Perhaps intimacy is not a constant state we maintain, but something that appears in episodes: moments when two people happen to meet each other with enough openness, presence, and willingness to be seen.

And when the moment passes, it doesn't become false.

> Neither makes the other untrue.

Yesterday's vulnerability can be real even if today brings silence. Yesterday's closeness can remain meaningful without demanding tomorrow's repetition.

Maybe the mistake is expecting every meaningful encounter to become a permanent condition.

> Maybe some intimacy is simply episodic.

It arrives when two people happen to inhabit the same emotional space.

Then life moves.

They return to themselves.

And perhaps that is not the end of intimacy.

Perhaps it is simply another state.
